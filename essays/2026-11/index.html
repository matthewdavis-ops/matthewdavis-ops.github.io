Monthly Essay

November 2026

The 6th of May, 1931. Harrisburg, Nebraska.

Far western edge of the panhandle.

Thin, dry air. Dust-heavy.

Crisp spring grass. Wind-scoured pine.

Waiting on a rain that isn’t coming.

Deep, sudden canyons choked with sagebrush.

Jagged bluffs cutting across a flat horizon.

A landscape sculpted by ancient winds.

A part of the country where cattle outnumber people eighty-five to one.

Sod houses. Barbed wire. A few dozen roofs huddled under a 5000′ sky.

Some 1,700 souls scattered across nearly 800 sq mi.

A frontier town at dawn. Uninsulated farmhouses. Frost melting off the pump handle. A woodstove smoking in a kitchen. A gravel road running straight into the empty blue until the earth drops away.

Western Nebraska. Silent. Immense. The edge of the high plains.

Larry Haun said the strongest memory of his childhood was that he was always cold.

Three feet from the kitchen stove? Still freezing. Whatever the temperature was outside, that was the temperature in the bedrooms. His mother warmed the sheets with a flat iron.

The nearest hardware store was 30 mi off. So, when something broke, they fixed it. He knew how to use tools before he knew how to read. He and his siblings made their toys out of apple and orange crates and built forts out of scrap lumber and nails they salvaged from a barn that had burned to the ground.

Sixteen years old, driving spikes into railroad ties.

At 17, he built his first house. A neighbor had ordered a two-bedroom out of a catalog. It came in by rail, in bundles, hauled to the site by a team of horses. Haun and one master carpenter unwrapped it and put it together.

Then he went looking for somewhere warm.

First stop? Albuquerque. Working for 87¢ an hour. Every cut on every piece of sheathing he made with a handsaw, because there was only one tablesaw on the whole job. That house took more than half a year to build. At the time? That was the industry standard.

Then, in 1950, he moved to Los Angeles. He called it paradise. His first year there, he never once wore a coat to work.

He had walked into the middle of something.

Postwar California needed houses faster than anyone knew how to build them.

They were coming.

A quarter of a million moved into the San Fernando Valley in five years.

And another 450,000 were already on the way.

A general contractor in those days could finish five or six houses a year. The old pace of construction? Simply couldn’t keep up.

So they stopped building houses one at a time.

Larry and his brothers Jim and Joe took framing at a flat rate. $90 to frame a 900 sq ft house on a slab. $120 for the three-bedroom. The three of them framed one a day. By 1953 they were one of the biggest framing outfits in Southern Cali, sending out a thousand sawblades a week to be resharpened.

The pace is honestly hard to picture. A carpenter was expected to hang eight doors in a day. Haun watched two men, Al and Royal Schieffer, hang nearly eight in an hour.

He got fast the way anybody gets fast. He looked at every single step and asked what it was actually for.

His hammer? Wrong. The standard sixteen-ounce curved-claw was built for finish work. He found a 28oz rigging hatchet with an 18″ handle, a tool meant for oil rigs, and loved everything about it except the blade, which kept catching him in the forehead. So he took it home, hacksawed the blade off, and had a friend weld a pair of claws onto it in a garage.

That is the California framing hammer. Invented by a man who got tired of bleeding.

The union didn’t care for it. In 1954 a business agent came out to the job site, asked to see the long-handled hammer, carried it over to a saw, and cut several inches off the handle to bring it into compliance.

Haun went home that night and put on a longer one.

He wasn’t fast because he hurried. Read his own rules and you find something stranger. A tolerance sheet. The mudsill can run a quarter inch short. The cap plate can run a quarter inch short. A wall a quarter inch out of plumb over eight feet is acceptable. But the rim joist has to land inside a sixteenth. The top plate has to be exact to a sixteenth. Kitchens and baths receive extra attention.

He called it building a house, not furniture.

That was not permission to be sloppy. That is a man who knew precisely where accuracy bought something and refused to spend it anywhere else. Anybody can be careful about everything for about a week. He was careful about the right things for sixty-three years.

And nobody ever saw any of it.

Framing gets covered up. Drywall goes over it. A family moves in, lives inside the work for 50-odd yrs, and never looks at it once. Nobody walks into a house and admires the top plate.

The grading came later, and it wasn’t gentle. Southern Cali gets earthquakes. By the time Haun died in 2011, the houses he and his brothers had thrown up in a day? The cheap ones. The tract houses. The ones nobody wrote about. Were 60 yrs old.

Hundreds of thousands of them are still standing.

Most craftsmen spend a lifetime protecting their advantage.

Larry Haun spent the second half of his career giving his away.

For 20 yrs he taught apprentices at a community college. He wrote more than twenty articles in Fine Homebuilding starting in 1989, walking total strangers through nailing, cutting, framing a roof, in language a beginner could follow. Books. Videos. A man whose entire professional advantage was being able to do it faster than you published exactly how he did it.

And when he retired, he kept building. Houses for Habitat for Humanity.

And wheelchair ramps for people in hospice care.

Sixty years of framing at full speed, and at the end, the smallest structure he would ever build, for somebody who was not going to need it very long. Same hands. Same standard. A ramp a dying man uses for three weeks still has to hold.

His last book came out in the fall of 2011. Not a how-to this time. A memoir, told through twelve houses, the ones he grew up in and the ones he built. He told his editors it made him nervous, being so unlike everything else he’d written. The final chapter is about building a 6′×8′ greenhouse out of salvaged materials.

He died on October 24, 2011. 80 yrs old. 63 yrs of work behind him.

We do a lot of that kind of work here.

Daily chamber safety check. Paying attention during a time out to make sure nothing goes in the chamber that shouldn’t. A note on a screening tool, written carefully for a question nobody has asked yet. The handoff done right at the end of a long shift, when nobody would know the difference. You almost never get to stand there and watch it hold.

But it holds or it doesn’t, and the grading always comes later.

So know which sixteenth of an inch matters, and spend yourself there.

Larry Haun spent 63 yrs building things he knew would disappear behind drywall.

Hundreds of thousands are still standing to this day.

That’s the thing about good hidden work.

If it was done right, people rely on it without ever giving it a second thought.
