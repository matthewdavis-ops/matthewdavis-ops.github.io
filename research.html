QComms Protocol Specification: A Quantum-Inspired Coordinate System for Semantic Space

Zenodo preprintDecember 22, 2025CC BY 4.0Davis, Matthew A.
Abstract

In 1679, Leibniz proposed the characteristica universalis — a universal symbolic language capable of representing all human thought through logical notation. The dream required infrastructure that would take 346 years to build: Malus’s polarization law (1809), Mueller’s transformation matrices (1943), Shannon’s information theory (1948), and the computational revolution culminating in transformer architectures and large language models trained on trillions of tokens. The semantic space Leibniz imagined now exists operationally, instantiated through large-scale statistical learning rather than formal symbolic construction. What remained missing was notation for specifying coordinates within it.

This paper presents Quantum Communication Theory (QCT) and its protocol specification, QComms — an application-layer addressing system for semantic space. Unlike traditional compression methodologies that reduce information density, QComms specifies coordinates — precise semantic locations that activate corresponding meaning constellations within the receiver’s substrate. Expansion is not decompression; it is recognition. The address activates what already exists.

Through a 15-week empirical study utilizing 29 specialized implementations across multiple platforms, we demonstrate 24–54% transmission efficiency gains (37% average) as a direct consequence of precise semantic addressing. Cross-platform validation confirms universal applicability independent of specific technological architectures. The protocol stack maps directly onto established OSI layers, positioning QComms as foundational infrastructure for semantic interoperability.

Results establish QCT as the operational realization of Leibniz’s 346-year-old vision: a coordinate system making meaning computable, transmissible, and universally addressable.

Tyndale: Semantic Addressing Protocol

Translation Yare Native Distributed Addressing Language Engine

IETF Internet-Draftdraft-davis-tyndale-00InformationalMarch 17, 2026M. Davis, Shared Health Services
Abstract

The protocol formalizes patterns that have emerged independently across human communication systems for 60,000 years: Aboriginal songlines, medical notation (Rx), ham radio Q-codes (QTH), maritime signals (SOS), and internet shorthand (1337, TL;DR).

This document specifies Tyndale, an application-layer semantic addressing protocol. Where traditional compression transmits reduced content (M → C → M), Tyndale transmits coordinates that the receiver expands locally (M → A, Σ(A) → M′). The receiver’s substrate already contains the meaning; transmission provides location, not payload. The selection formula tau = (M / S) x R x G optimizes for meaning preserved per signal spent (M/S), resilience across expression systems (R), and cognitive alignment with receiver processing (G).

Bandwidth-constrained environments — disaster response networks, degraded infrastructure, deep space communications — require semantic transmission under conditions where traditional compression fails. When every bit costs power, time, or lives, communication systems need a different primitive.

Taft’s teletype (1909). Voyager 1 (160 bps @ 15 billion miles). iPhone. Same protocol.

Tyndale is to natural language what DNS is to IP addresses.

The mathematics describes how meaning moves.
