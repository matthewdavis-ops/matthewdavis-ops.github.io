## What CEO ↔ CNA Means

In a hospital, the CEO runs the show while a CNA, a certified nursing assistant, is often the person delivering direct patient care. Two very different jobs and two people with very different days. Between them there are a slew of C-Suite individuals (COOs, CFOs, CMOs, CNOs), providers, program directors, billing and coding professionals, nursing staff, and technicians.

CEO ↔ CNA (read it as "CEO to CNA") is the principle that communication should remain accessible from the executive suite to the bedside. The goal is not to simplify ideas, but to make them understandable without treating anyone as less capable than anyone else. Plain words that carry the full weight of an idea, so the CEO of a hospital doesn't feel talked down to and a CNA doesn't need a glossary and the message lands with everyone in the gap between them equally.

It doesn't mean everyone gets the same email. A document everyone shares, like a form, a reference sheet or a blog post, is written once, for all of them. A message is shaped to what each reader needs: the executive gets the overview, the clinical team gets the full body. What changes is the payload. What never changes is who's talking, or how the reader is treated.

### The Six Moves

In practice it comes down to six moves:

1. Don't talk down to anyone. Nobody should feel lectured, and nobody should need a glossary.
2. Don't step on toes. The problem is almost never a person. Name the gap, not the culprit.
3. Lead the reader. Walk them to the point one step at a time instead of announcing the answer and asking them to trust it.
4. Make every claim checkable. The data and the sources go on the page, so the reader can verify what they were told without asking me.
5. Relocate, don't delete. When a detail would trip the least-specialized reader, it moves one step back, to training, a reference sheet or a footnote, where the specialists already look. It doesn't disappear.
6. Let the reader draw the conclusion. Lay the cards down. People defend the conclusions they reach themselves.

### Where It Came From

I didn't learn this in the healthcare industry, but sometimes I wonder where the CEO ↔ CNA methodology came from, and the honest answer is that I don't know.

But I can point to influences.

- William Stafford taught me that ordinary language can carry serious ideas.
- HTML and BASIC taught me that instructions fail when important information is missing.
- Drafting and AutoCAD taught me to leave enough information behind that the next person can build from the foundation.
- Shop class taught me that measurements only work when everyone arrives at the same answer.
- Drawing comics taught me that every frame has to explain itself.

None of them invented the doctrine, but looking back, they all seem to have been teaching the same lesson from different directions.

The burden belongs to the thought, not the vocabulary.

Then I spent nearly twenty years working beside people whose days looked nothing alike: hospital CEOs, Directors of Nursing, CNAs, auditors, plant managers, revenue cycle professionals, technicians, and temp-to-hire crews.

Along the way I noticed something.

Most communication problems aren't intelligence problems. They're translation problems.

The people involved usually understand their jobs just fine. What they don't always understand is everyone else's.

It's hard to write for someone you've never worked beside.

Eventually it became my house writing standard with a one-sentence test: could a board member read it without feeling condescended to, and could a CNA read it without a glossary? The rigor is in everything from the delivery, to citations, to a sensible order that carries the weight of complex topics, so the sentences can stay plain.

## Where It Shows Up

Most communication doctrines break down the moment they're asked to leave the page.

They work in an email but not on a form. In a presentation but not in a conversation. In theory but not under pressure.

CEO ↔ CNA survives because it isn't really a writing rule. It's a translation rule.

The medium changes. The audience changes. The problem changes.

The questions? Stay the same.

Who needs this information? What do they need to do with it? And what would force them to stop and ask someone else for help?

Whether I'm writing an email, building a tool, researching a problem, or drafting a blog post, the goal is always the same: make sure the next person can move forward without me standing beside them.

To me, they're the same problem wearing different clothes.

### In an Email

On a busy day I’ve sent as many as eighteen partner emails, each one written for whoever was going to open it, from hospital executives to clinical technicians.

The same news rarely goes out the same way twice. Take a single Medicare payment change. A hospital C-Suite executive needs to know what it costs their facility, with a dollar amount attached, and the source next to it. A program director needs to know what will be different come Monday morning. The providers, nursing staff, and technicians need to know what to document in the patient’s record as well as where to find the rule.

Three emails, same facts, same sources, and nothing withheld from anyone. Sure, they might have different first sentences, because each reader opens the email asking a different question, but the end goals are the same: accessibility, honesty, and clarity.

The harder part of doing such a job is not sending the emails that never actually go out of the drafts folder at all. Plenty of news is true, but it may not be worth anyone's attention or would needlessly rile people up. Others are true but would land as blame. Part of writing correspondence for every reader is deciding which readers shouldn't be interrupted at all.

### In a Tool

Take the hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) screening system¹ I rebuilt earlier this year. It’s for a therapy where a patient breathes 100% pure oxygen in a hard-sided pressurized chamber, and Medicare pays only when a long list of underlying conditions and coverage criteria is documented in a patient’s record. Initially, six indications shared one form with Yes and No boxes and a narrow strip for comments. Most rows had nowhere to write a date or a source, so the hyperbaric technologists wrote on the back of the form.

The problem wasn't that people didn't know what they were doing. The problem was that the form assumed they could fit the entire story into the space provided.

The fix wasn't a better form. It was a different idea of who the form was for.

That one form became seven, each built around one indication or one closely related group of indications, so nobody had to scroll past what didn't apply to their patient. Every criterion got room to write, a place for the date, and a plain sentence saying what counts.

The instructions at the top of every tool say the whole doctrine in four short sentences:

> Mark a criterion met only when the record documents it. Otherwise, flag it as a gap. If not applicable, write N/A in Comments. Never leave blanks.

The tool doesn't decide whether a patient qualifies. It simply shows what's documented, what's missing, and where the gaps are. The decision belongs to the provider.

The rest of the doctrine shows up line by line. Medical terms keep their clinical name and get a plain explanation next to it, like "Telangiectasia (spider veins / broken blood vessels)." The provider isn't talked down to and a technician doesn't feel left behind. Boundaries are written out instead of assumed: "Gauze alone does not count." And the best line in the set is from the osteomyelitis (bone-infection) screening tool:

> Count the weeks and write the number here — do not leave a reviewer to do the arithmetic.

That line is written for someone the technician will never meet: the reviewer, an auditor from Medicare, who opens the chart months or years later without anyone in the room to explain what happened.

The technician is documenting for today's patient.

The reviewer is reconstructing the story later from whatever was written.

CEO ↔ CNA applies there too. Different jobs. Different goals. Different perspectives. The same responsibility to communicate clearly.

Writing for every reader includes the ones you'll never meet. Including the auditor.

### In a Blog Post

Earlier this year, during one week in July, three vendors emailed me or colleagues with nearly identical pitches: physician-use peptides, third-party tested, a new revenue stream for our partners with no heavy lift. During that same week, a federal advisory committee was considering whether several of those compounds should be added to the 503A Bulks List.

The easy blog post was a warning. Point at the gray market, let everyone feel briefly superior, and move on with the day. The problem was that it wouldn't have taught the reader anything. It also wouldn't have been true. Peptide medicines are real. Insulin is a peptide. GLP-1 medications are peptides. There is legitimate peptide research happening in wound care right now.

The question wasn't whether peptides were good or bad. The question was whether the sales pitch was telling the whole story.

So the post said what it was right up front: This wasn't a warning. It was a translation.²

From there, it walked the reader through four phrases from the sales pitch, what each one was actually doing, and then through the FDA's own published file³ on one of the compounds, one finding at a time, each one cited. Every claim had a source beside it. Every conclusion was built in public.

And then, near the end, came the most important sentence in the entire article:

> Although, none of those facts, by themselves, answer whether TB-500 should, or should not, be compounded.

That's the doctrine in practice.

The goal wasn't to tell the reader what to think. The goal was to put every card on the table so they could reach their own conclusion.

A warning? Tells the reader what to think. A translation shows them how to think through it.

No one gets called a fool. No one gets talked down to. Every claim has a source beside it. The reader is led, not pushed, toward being able to read the next pitch on their own.

The company blog I've written since 2020 runs on that rule.

One article written that way, on medical documentation,⁴ received a MarCom Platinum Award in 2025⁵, judged by communications professionals with no stake in wound care.

### In a Problem

The doctrine isn't only for writing. It's also how I test whether I understand something.

If I can't explain an idea clearly enough that the newest person on the team can understand it, then one of two things is true. Either I don't understand it well enough myself. Or there's a gap. Both are worth knowing.

The screening tool for diabetic foot ulcers needed to help a technician determine whether a patient's record established Medicare's coverage requirements for treatment. One requirement looked simple enough. The patient had to have a wound classified as a Wagner Grade III or higher. Somewhere between rebuilding the screening system and writing about it, I went looking for the definition in the national coverage determination (20.29).⁶ It named the grade but never defined it.

Turns out, the definition lived somewhere else.

Eventually the trail led me to a 2002 CMS decision memo (CAG-00060N)⁷ that laid out all six Wagner grades in detail while simultaneously stating, in its own words, that it was not a coverage determination. In fact, it was a decision memorandum stating CMS’s intent to issue an NCD.

The coverage criteria depended on a definition that wasn't actually in the coverage determination. And the document containing the definition said it wasn't actually a determination at all.

Building a screening tool for hyperbaric technicians to use on a daily basis meant I couldn't stop at citing the national coverage determination. I had to keep following the trail until I found where Medicare was actually getting its language from.

The result ended up in the screening tool.

Instead of simply saying "Wagner Grade III," the tool explains what establishes it, what findings qualify, what evidence supports those findings, and what does not qualify on its own.

The goal wasn't to make the decision for the user. The goal was to show my work well enough that a technician could retrace my steps and redo the research on their own.

That's the doctrine again. Cite where the data on the page came from and leave the conclusion to the reader.

The same habit keeps me off people's toes when the problem involves people.

In the blog post that came out of that project⁸, a patient sees three physicians over eight days. One documents the wound in a primary care note that doesn’t establish a wound grade. Another uses the University of Texas classification system. A third uses WIfI. Every note is clinically correct. Every note answers the question it was written to answer. And yet none of them fully establish the specific coverage requirement Medicare needs for the patient to qualify for HBOT.

The easy conclusion is that somebody missed something. The more useful conclusion fit in a single sentence:

> Each note answers the question it was asked.

Nobody was wrong.

The problem was translation. And translation is a problem you can solve without turning anyone into a villain.

## Why It Travels

Every organization has a CEO and a CNA, even if nobody calls them that. The person responsible for deciding what happens next and the person responsible for making it happen.

Most communication? Stops there. CEO ↔ CNA doesn't. Because there's a third reader most communication forgets: the one who picks it up later.

An auditor. A new hire. A customer. The person inheriting your job. The person opening a file six months after everyone else has forgotten the conversation.

That third reader is who the doctrine is really for.

If a page works for someone reading it cold, with nobody in the room to explain it, it will usually work for the people using it today.

The subject changes from job to job. Reimbursement rules. Furnace data. Hospital floor plans. Vendor sales pitches. The method doesn't:

- Write for the person who reads it last.
- Put everything they need to check it on the page.
- Let them reach the conclusion themselves.

The technicians who wrote on the back of the form weren't cutting corners. They were working around a page that had no room for the truth.

Most communication problems work the same way. The important part isn't hidden. It's stranded.

On the back of the page.

In the attachment nobody opens.

In the meeting the right person wasn't invited to.

In the explanation everyone assumes somebody else already knows.

CEO ↔ CNA is the habit of checking for that before anything goes out. Who reads this first, who reads it last, and can both of them find what matters without rebuilding the thing on their own?

If the answer is yes? It's ready.
